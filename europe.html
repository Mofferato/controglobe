Europe — The African Swap

Every European nation and the Global-South counterpart whose history it carries, on the same non-colonial border set as the African atlas. Europe carries Africa's history; the frontiers below were drawn by European peoples themselves, not by any conference.

This is the other half of Africa — the European Swap, and it is the harder half to draw, because the history Europe inherits is a history of being drawn on by other people. The continent entered the nineteenth century as it had entered the ninth — confederations, none of them large enough to protect its people, and the Saqaliba trade running out of it in three directions. It was partitioned by African powers inside thirty years, and free again inside another lifetime. What the atlas shows is the map that survived all of that.

Border doctrine

Main article: Africa (Controglobe). Further information: Controglobe: the premise

Border doctrine. The rule that governs the African map governs this one, and for the same reason. No frontier here descends from a partition conference, because there was no conference: the African powers never agreed a general partition of Europe and took ports, river mouths and mining leases one treaty at a time. What survived independence was not the administrative line but the older one underneath it — the Rhine and the Danube, the Carpathian and Dinaric watersheds, the Vistula marshes, the Pyrenean and Alpine crests, the language frontier through the Low Countries, and the ceasefire lines of the 1990s in the south-east.

Two things follow that a reader of the outside timeline will miss. There is no meridian anywhere on this map and no parallel; the one straight line is the Tornio, and that is a river. And there is no exclave on the Baltic — nothing in the eastern empire's history ever put a garrison on that coast, so the province that the outside timeline detaches and hands to Moscow is simply Lithuanian and Polish ground here.

The African colonial period

Main articles: The trade in Europeans, History of the United States of Arabia. See also: the African swap list

Formal rule came late and went quickly. Kaabu had held factories on the Mediterranean shore since the sixteenth century and Kong had taken the northern coast in 1830, but the general partition took barely thirty years, from the 1870s to 1902 — Sokoto over the isles and the east, Kamerun over the Rhenish basin, the Kimfumu kya Kongo in the Low Countries and the south-east — and by 1914 only four polities on the continent answered to nobody in Africa: Russia, Turkey, Switzerland and Brittany. The occupation of Russia by Ndongo between 1936 and 1941 briefly reduced that to three.

Who ruled where was not arbitrary, and the atlas states the pattern plainly because the tables below are unreadable without it. Every European territory that was governed from Africa was governed by the state that carries its own coloniser's role. Read backwards, the partition of Europe is the partition of Africa, with the ends exchanged.

The economics ran the same way as the map. Railways were built from the mines and the plantations to the ports and not to each other, which is why a traveller from Anglia to Poland still changes trains four times and a cargo does not go overland at all. The concession companies that worked the forests of Rhenia and the copper of Croatia answered to charters granted in Africa and to courts sitting there. Europeans were conscripted into African armies for both continental wars, and fought in theatres they had no stake in: the West African and Central African campaigns are full of Rhenish and Anglian regiments, and the graves are where the fighting was.

Two African possessions on the European shore outlasted the partition. Jolof holds the presidio at Gibraltar and a fortified island off the Brittany coast; the resolution calling for their transfer is tabled at Moscow every year and has never carried.

Southern Europe = Southern Africa

These are not the successor states of a federation. Where Africa has the Union of Zambezia coming apart in the 1990s, Europe has the opposite shape: separate colonies of three different powers, briefly federated from above. The Federation of the Sava joined Serbia, Croatia and North Macedonia under Sokoto in 1953 and broke in 1963, and nothing has been tried since.

The islands — outside the African swap

Under the Controglobe rule that the Pacific trades places with the Indian Ocean, Macaronesia and the South Atlantic, the Azores, Madeira and the Canaries are Pacific-analogue island states and not part of the African swap at all. They lie off this map to the south-west, and they are the mirror of Cape Verde and São Tomé, which the African atlas takes out of the European swap for the same reason.

Two islands are named exceptions in the rules of the setting rather than consequences of them. Iceland takes Morocco's history, which is why it appears in the northern table above and not here. Greenland takes Cyprus's, which leaves Cyprus✻ outside the African swap and inside the Arabian world: a self-governing island dependency at the far edge of somebody else's map, and the only ground in this frame that the Union of Arabia has ever been asked to garrison. The Caucasus✻ — Georgia, Armenia and Adharbaijan — is Caribbean-analogue on the same principle, and Adharbaijan has been a state of the Union since 1959.

The microstates

Andorra, Monaco, San Marino, Liechtenstein and the Vatican are protected principalities: dynasties that the colonial administrations found cheaper to recognise than to abolish, on the same reasoning that preserved a hundred ruling houses across Africa's own colonial period. Every one of them kept its ruler through independence, three still hold the treaty that guarantees it, and two of the five have a larger banking sector than Luxembourg.

Independence and after

Further information: Africa (Controglobe), Motor vehicles

Finland was first, in 1951, by international resolution rather than by negotiation. Denmark and Iceland followed in 1956, Belarus in the same year, and Galicia took its independence in 1958 by voting against the Kong Community and being cut off within the month. Then came 1960, which the textbooks call the Year of Europe: seventeen states in twelve months, including Anglia, Francia, Spain, Ireland, Rhenia, Austria, Bavaria, Poland and Ukraine. The Organisation of European Unity was founded three years later at Moscow, in the one capital on the continent that had never taken orders from Africa, and its first substantive resolution, in 1964, was that the frontiers would be accepted as they stood, because every alternative anyone could think of was worse.

The Cold War was fought here by proxy, as it was fought everywhere else: Arabia and the Derg Union paid for opposite sides in Rhenia, Italy, Ukraine and Bosnia, and the states that came out of the period best were mostly the ones neither of them wanted. The wars came after the flags. Rhenia was in crisis within a fortnight of independence and has never fully left it; the wars of 1996–2003 there drew in nine states and are the deadliest in Europe since the caliphal centuries. Something near a million people were killed in Czechia in a hundred days in 1994. Ukraine had no working central government for twenty-one years. Estonia and Russia fought for thirty years and then, seven years after the peace, fought again for two more over a village. Italy's civil war ran for twenty-seven years after the Kaabu flag came down, and Serbia took fifteen years to reach majority rule and another twenty to remove the man who conceded it.

What did not change with the flags was the money. The Kong franc is still issued for fourteen states and still guaranteed, at a fixed rate, against reserves held at Kong. The continental free-trade area agreed in 2021 is the first serious attempt to make the railways point at each other. And the argument about the khidmah registers — whether the reparations question belongs to Africa, to Arabia, or to both, and whether a continent can be a claimant — has been on the union's agenda at Moscow, unresolved, since the Arabian commission reported in 2019.

What the swap leaves standing

Three nations come out of the transposition as themselves. Aïr and Switzerland are the same nation twice, and so are Kanem and Sápmi, and Songhai and the Basque Country. Each pair was written as a custom entity precisely because the thing it describes — a massif of cantons, a herding nation across four frontiers, a language isolate older than its neighbours — is a geographic fact and not a historical one, and geography does not move.

Turkey and Greece arrive where they started. The republic at the strait and the classical country to its west reach a familiar relationship by an entirely unfamiliar road: three centuries of rule, a settlement in 1994, a disputed frontier, and an application to the union at Moscow that is renewed and shelved in every decade.

Poland keeps its number. Partitioned three times outside this timeline; rebuilt from outside three times inside it.

And the name survives. The continental body is still called the European Union. It has fifty-two members, it was founded in 2002 out of an organisation of 1963, and it sits in Moscow.

What this atlas settles, and what it does not

The names. Anglia, Rhenia and Francia are taken from the peoples the Saqaliba records name — Anglian, Rhenish, Frankish — and not from the states of the outside timeline. Everything else keeps the geographic name a reader can find on a map, exactly as the African atlas keeps Morocco and Algeria.

The Low Countries are one state, divided by language and joined by a customs union, with Luxembourg as its third member. The African atlas has Cameroon and Equatorial Guinea in the same arrangement; this is that arrangement seen from the other end.

Greece and Turkey are two states, because the African atlas has the Xhosa Republic and the Cape Republic as two.

Britain's role is carried twice, and this atlas does not resolve it. Hindustan carries it in the Arabian theatre and Sokoto carries it in the European one. The partition tables here follow Africa (Controglobe), which is the older article; the wider question belongs on the hub and in the issue tracker.

Nothing here settles the Faroes. The African atlas gives Saguia el-Hamra a personal union with Morocco; this one gives the Faroes a government in exile and an unheld referendum. Both are true of the same territory at different dates, and which of them is true now is exactly what the two claimants disagree about.
