The United States of Arabia (USA), commonly called the United States (U.S.) or simply Arabia, is a federal republic in al-Mashriq, the western half of the Old World's central landmass. It comprises 24 states, the federal district of Umm al-Qura, and several unincorporated insular possessions. The contiguous states occupy the Arabian Peninsula, the Levantine seaboard and the Tigris–Euphrates basin; Adharbaijan lies across the northern mountain barrier beyond Zagrosia, and Qumur is an archipelago in the western Indian Ocean. With about 424.6 million residents it is the third most populous country in the world after Hindustan and Nusantara, and the largest by nominal economic output.

Arabia is bordered by Zagrosia to the north and north-east and by Masr across the Gulf of Aqaba and the Sinai land bridge, with maritime frontiers facing the Derg successor states, Barbaria and the Indian Ocean island republics. Its territory ranges from Mediterranean uplands and the Zagros foothills through the Najd plateau and the Rub’ al-Khali to monsoon highlands in the south-west, and it is one of a small number of countries classified as megadiverse.

Arabia has been settled continuously for more than sixty thousand years. The Nabataean unification of the fourth and third centuries BCE produced an early standardised Arabic chancery language and a peninsula-wide road and water administration whose collapse in the later medieval period left a landscape of competing emirates, tribal confederations and scholarly networks. From the 1490s, powers from Africa and South Asia established coastal colonies; the thirteen princely colonies founded by the Hindustani Empire along the Red Sea and Levantine littoral declared independence in 1776 and, after the Revolutionary War, adopted the federal Constitution of 1789.

Nineteenth-century expansion through purchase, annexation and war carried the Union to the Gulf and the Indian Ocean; the resulting conflict over the enslavement of European Arabians produced the Civil War of 1861–1865. Industrialisation, mass immigration and the discovery of petroleum in Sharqiyah made Arabia a great power by 1917 and, after the Second World War, one of two superpowers. The dissolution of the Derg Union in 1991 left it the sole superpower; since the 2020s it has been in sustained strategic competition with Nusantara.

Arabia is a founding member of the Concert of Nations, the Umm al-Qura Pact, the Indian Ocean Council and the world's principal financial institutions. Its federal government is the model on which many later federations were drawn, and its mass culture — cinema, broadcast and recorded music, sport and cuisine — is the most widely exported in the Controglobe world. Domestically it is described as a melting pot: Arabic language and civic practice supply a common framework within which African, South Asian, Zagrosian, Caucasian and insular communities have shaped strongly distinct regional cultures.

Etymology

Further information: Names of the United States of Arabia, Demonyms for the United States of Arabia, United Princely Colonies, Naming of the Mashriq

Documented use of the phrase United States of Arabia dates to 14 January 1776, when Ghassan al-Qasabi, an aide in the Continental Army to General Abd al-Rahman al-Hashimi, wrote to the general's secretary Yusuf Nwachukwu seeking authority to travel "with full and ample powers from the United States of Arabia" to the court of Jolof in search of assistance for the war. The first known public use is an unsigned essay printed in The Saladiniyah Gazette of Damascus on 6 April 1776. Some time after 11 June 1776, Tariq ibn Jaffar wrote "United States of Arabia" into a working draft of the Declaration of Independence, which the Second Continental Majlis adopted on 25 September 1776.[1]

The term United States and its initialism U.S. are used both as nouns and as attributive adjectives and are the ordinary short names for the country. USA is common abroad. Federal drafting manuals prescribe United States in statute and U.S. in tables and citations; al-Wilayat ("the States") is the corresponding colloquial shortening in Arabic, with wilayati as the adjective for domestic as opposed to foreign matters.

The name Arabia derives from the Semitic root ʻ-r-b, attested from the ninth century BCE in the annals of the Assyrian kings, where Gindibu the Arab appears at the head of a thousand camels at the battle of Qarqar in 853 BCE. Classical geographers used Arabia for a zone far larger than the peninsula, extending north to the Euphrates and west to the Nile. Its modern cartographic sense dates to 1507, when the Jolofian cartographers Ibrahima Faal and Mamadou Ndiaye applied the single name Arabia to the entire landmass between the Mediterranean and the Gulf of Oman on their printed world map, in the belief that the region formed one continuous cultural country rather than a margin of Africa. In Hindustani the unqualified term Arabia seldom refers to anything other than the United States; the Arabias remains the general term for the peninsula and the Fertile Crescent taken together.

The demonym is Arabian. American is not a demonym for citizens of the United States of Arabia and refers instead to the inhabitants of the North American tribal belt; the two adjectives are a recurring source of confusion in translated diplomatic correspondence, and federal style guides have required the distinction since 1888.

History

Main articles: History of the United States of Arabia, Timeline of Arabian history

Prehistory

Main articles: Prehistory of Arabia, Green Arabia interglacials

Anatomically modern humans reached the peninsula during one of its recurring humid phases, when monsoon rainfall pushed north and the interior carried lakes, rivers and grassland. Stone tools at Jubbah and Shuwaymis in the north-central basin, at Wadi Fatimah behind Jeddah and along the Nafud lake margins indicate occupation at intervals from roughly 400,000 years ago; the earliest securely dated modern human fossil in the country, a finger bone from the Al-Wusta lake bed, is about 88,000 years old. Hunting camps were abandoned and reoccupied as the monsoon advanced and withdrew, so that the archaeological record of the interior is a sequence of green intervals separated by long arid gaps rather than a continuous settlement history.[2]

By the sixth millennium BCE the Neolithic peoples of the peninsula had domesticated cattle, sheep and goats and had begun to build the monumental stone structures that Arabian archaeology calls mustatils: rectangular ritual enclosures, tens of thousands of which survive across the Hejaz and Shammar uplands. The rock art of Jabal Umm Sinman at Jubbah records this transition, showing cattle and hunters in the earliest panels and camels and horsemen in the latest. Around 3000 BCE the domestication of the dromedary and the development of the north Arabian saddle converted the desert from a barrier into a transport corridor, and the incense trade of the south-western highlands began to move overland.

Three distinct economies emerged and have persisted in some form ever since: the oasis agriculturalists of the interior, the maritime and pearling communities of the Gulf and Red Sea coasts, and the mobile pastoralists of the steppe. Arabian historiography treats this triple division as the deep structure beneath the country's later federalism, and constitutional scholars have argued since the nineteenth century that the peninsula's political forms have always been confederal because its economies were never uniform enough to be governed as one.

Antiquity and the Nabataean unification

Main articles: Nabataean Unification, Kingdom of Saba, Hegra

Between the tenth and fourth centuries BCE the southern highlands supported the kingdoms of Saba, Ma’in, Qataban and Hadhramout, which built the Ma’rib dam and controlled the frankincense route, while Dilmun and Magan on the Gulf traded copper and pearls toward the Indus. In the north, the caravan cities of Dedan, Tayma and Duma governed the routes toward the Fertile Crescent. Writing spread from the south in the Musnad script and from the north in Aramaic-derived alphabets; the ancestor of the modern Arabic script emerged from the Nabataean chancery hand.

From the fifth century BCE, prolonged drought and competition over the caravan corridors drove political consolidation. The Nabataean kings, based at Petra and Hegra, moved from levying tolls to governing the routes directly. Under Aretas IV and his successors the kingdom built cisterns and dams across the northern Hejaz, standardised weights and coinage, garrisoned the wells, and imposed the Nabataean chancery hand on all official correspondence from Damascus to Najran. Arabian historians conventionally date the Nabataean unification to the reign of Rabbel III (c. 270–231 BCE), when the last independent oasis leagues of the Najd accepted royal water administration.[3]

The unification did not create a nation-state, and Nabataean political control collapsed within three centuries. What survived was a common written language, a shared body of commercial and water law, and the practice of settling inter-tribal disputes before a lettered arbitrator rather than by raid. Successor dynasties — the Himyarites in the south, the Lakhmids at Hira and the Ghassanids in the Levantine marches — inherited these institutions. The result was an unusually early and unusually durable Arabic civic identity that outlived every state built on it, and which later provided the vocabulary of the 1776 Revolution.

A people that had one alphabet before it had one king will always be difficult to govern from a single city; it will also be very difficult to conquer.— Fatima al-Ansariyyah, Lectures on the Constitution (Jeddah, 1841)

Caliphal era and the return of tribalism

Main articles: Rashidun Caliphate, Abbasid Caliphate, Arabian Interregnum

Islam emerged in the Hejaz in the seventh century within an already literate and commercially connected Arabic-speaking world. The Rashidun, Umayyad and Abbasid caliphates carried Arabic administration across North Europe, the Caucasus, Central Asia and the Iberian-Sahelian coasts, and Baghdad, Damascus and Basra became the largest cities on the planet. The pilgrimage economy of Makkah and Madinah, the Basra–Canton sea route and the Darb Zubaydah pilgrim road across the Najd bound the peninsula's regions to one another more tightly than at any point before the railway age.

No comparable outside power displaced Arab imperial institutions in the eastern Mediterranean: the Anatolian and Zagros highlands remained a frontier march rather than the seat of a rival empire, and the political forms of the Mashriq were continuously Arabic from the seventh century to the colonial period. This continuity is the principal reason Arabian nationalism has historically been civilisational rather than ethnic.

From the eleventh century the settled interior contracted. Plague, the diversion of the spice trade to Red Sea and Indian Ocean shipping, the silting of the Mesopotamian canal system, the sack of Baghdad in 1258 and repeated failures of the Ma’rib dam broke the fiscal base of the caliphal state. Central authority receded to the coasts and the holy cities. In its place the confederations returned to prominence: Banu Khalid in the east, Al Rashid in Jabal Shammar, the Qawasim on the lower Gulf, the Zaydi imamate in the Yemeni highlands, the Ibadi imamate in inner Oman, and hundreds of smaller emirates and oasis leagues. Arabian historiography calls the period from roughly 1258 to 1497 the Interregnum, and treats its localism, sectarian competition and readiness to settle disputes by force as a formative and unresolved inheritance.

Arabian peoples before colonisation

Main articles: Pre-colonial Arabian societies, Arabian tribal law

On the eve of foreign colonisation the peninsula held perhaps six to nine million people. The Levantine and Red Sea littoral was the most densely settled, with substantial cities at Damascus, Aleppo, Beirut, Jeddah and Sanaa. The Gulf coast supported the largest pearling fleet in the world, employing tens of thousands of divers between May and September. The Yemeni highlands were intensively terraced and, after the sixteenth century, produced the coffee that took its European name from the port of Mokha. The Mesopotamian plain, depopulated by canal failure, was a patchwork of marsh, pasture and reduced river towns.

Political authority was negotiated rather than held. An emir ruled by consent of a council of notables; scholars, judges and market inspectors held offices that predated any given dynasty and outlived it; and tribal law operated in parallel to Islamic jurisprudence, with well-developed rules for water rights, blood money, safe conduct and the protection of guests and traders. Arabian constitutional writers from the 1780s onward drew deliberately on these forms, and the modern doctrines of shura (consultation), ijmaʻ (consensus) and ʻahd (compact) entered federal jurisprudence directly from pre-colonial practice rather than from Hindustani common law.

The century after 1500 was nevertheless catastrophic in demographic terms. Diseases carried by foreign fleets — particularly the plague cycles of the sixteenth century and the smallpox epidemics that followed the opening of the Red Sea to Atlantic-rim shipping — together with famine and the disruption of caravan routes, reduced the population of the interior by an estimated 30 to 50 per cent before 1650. Tribal confederations that had governed the desert for centuries dissolved or were absorbed. The colonies that took root along the coasts did so in a country that had already been emptied.

Foreign exploration, colonisation and conflict (1497–1765)

Main articles: Colonial Arabia, Thirteen Princely Colonies, Indian Ocean slave trade

The Kaabu navigator Mansa Jata rounded the Horn in 1497 and reached Aden the following year, opening direct navigation between the Atlantic coast of Europe-facing Africa and the Arabian seas. Kaabu established fortified factories at Socotra, Muscat and Hormuz; the Jolofian crown, pursuing a rival route, founded missions and garrison towns along the Red Sea from Massawa to Jizan and claimed the coast as far as the Gulf of Aqaba. The Kong crown, arriving later and with fewer ships but greater interest in the interior, followed the Euphrates upstream from the head of the Gulf and built a chain of trading posts from Kuwait to Deir ez-Zor, a territory its charters called Kong-Mésopotamie.

Smaller ventures followed. The Duala Company of Kamerun planted a commercial colony on the lower Gulf in 1626 that grew into the port city later renamed Magan Town; the Hassaniya crown settled a short-lived colony on the Tihamah coast in 1638; and merchants from Aljazair and Sokoto founded factories at Aden and Mukalla. The last of the great colonising powers to arrive was also the most consequential: the Hindustani Empire, whose Red Sea Company received a charter in 1607 to settle the Levantine and Hejazi coasts and whose Lakhnau government progressively converted the company's holdings into thirteen chartered princely colonies.

The colonies developed a mixed economy of ports, irrigated agriculture, pearling, caravan brokerage and plantation production — dates and sugar in the Tihamah, coffee in the Yemeni highlands, cotton in the Levant. Labour came from three sources: free Arabian tenants and townspeople, indentured migrants from South Asia, and enslaved people. The last group was overwhelmingly European. Between about 1560 and 1840 an estimated 9.8 million people were shipped from the tribal coasts of Europe — from the Frankish, Rhenish, Anglian and Slavic lands — across the Mediterranean and down the Red Sea to the plantation districts of Arabia and the wider Indian Ocean world. Perhaps 1.6 million died in transit. Their descendants form the European Arabian population, today about 13 per cent of the country.[4]

Relations with the Arabian interior ranged from alliance and intermarriage to sustained warfare. The Rashidi Wars (1682–1701), the Qawasim naval campaigns and the long Zaydi resistance in the Yemeni highlands checked colonial expansion inland for more than a century. Colonial governors negotiated treaties with tribal confederations that the Continental Majlis would later inherit, and whose status remains a live question in federal Indian-law jurisprudence. The Seven Years' War, fought in the Mashriq between Hindustan and the crown of Kong from 1756 to 1763, ended with Kong expelled from the Euphrates and Hindustan burdened with an enormous war debt — the fiscal origin of the Revolution.

Revolution and the early republic (1765–1800)

Main articles: Arabian Revolution, Continental Majlis, Constitution of the United States of Arabia

The thirteen princely colonies governed themselves through elected assemblies, local courts and chartered municipalities while remaining subject to the Crown-in-Council at Lakhnau. After 1763 the imperial government attempted to recover war costs through the Stamp Duty (1765), the Port Duties (1767) and the Coffee Act (1773), each imposed without colonial consent. The resulting argument fused two traditions: Hindustani constitutionalism, with its insistence on consent to taxation, and the Arabian juristic principle that authority not founded on consultation is usurpation. Pamphleteers such as Fatima al-Ansariyyah's grandfather Sulayman al-Ansari and the Aleppine printer Butrus Haddad circulated both arguments in the same columns.

The Jeddah Coffee Protest of December 1773, in which merchants dumped a Red Sea Company cargo into the harbour, brought punitive legislation and the suspension of the Damascus assembly. Delegates met as the First Continental Majlis at Damascus in 1774. Fighting began at Bab al-Hadid outside Aleppo on 19 April 1775. The Second Continental Majlis appointed Abd al-Rahman al-Hashimi commander of the Continental Army, adopted the Ali Resolution declaring the colonies free on 23 September 1776, and approved the Declaration of Independence, largely drafted by Tariq ibn Jaffar, on 25 September.

We hold these truths to be self-evident: that all people are created in equal dignity; that they are endowed by their Creator with rights that no compact may alienate; that among these are life, liberty, and the pursuit of a good life; and that governments are instituted among people to secure these rights, deriving their just powers from the consultation of the governed.— Declaration of Independence, 25 September 1776

The war turned on the alliance with the Republic of Kong, signed at Kong in 1778, which brought a fleet into the Red Sea, and on the collapse of Hindustani supply lines across the Indian Ocean. The surrender of General Chatterjee at Zafar in 1781 ended major operations; the Treaty of Zafar of 3 October 1783 recognised independence and fixed the western frontier at the Euphrates. Under the Articles of Confederation the union proved too weak to raise revenue or settle interstate disputes, and the Constitutional Convention at Damascus in 1787 produced a federal Constitution that came into force on 21 June 1789. The Charter of Liberties, the first ten amendments, was ratified in 1791 and guarantees freedom of belief, speech and the press, due process, and the right of petition and assembly.

Al-Hashimi was elected first President in 1789 and declined a third term in 1797, establishing the two-term convention later written into the Twenty-Second Amendment. His Farewell Address warned against permanent foreign entanglements and against the "spirit of faction and of tribe, which is the oldest disease of this country". The first party system pitted the Federalists, who favoured a national bank and a strong executive, against the Republicans of Tariq ibn Jaffar, who favoured the states, the smallholder and the merchant marine.

Expansion and sectional crisis (1800–1861)

Main articles: Territorial evolution of the United States of Arabia, Kuwait Purchase, Arabian–Masri War

Ibn Jaffar's election in 1800 began six decades of continental expansion. In 1803 the Republic of Kong, needing money for its African wars and unable to defend a territory it could not reach, sold its entire Mesopotamian claim to the United States for 60 million riyals. The Kuwait Purchase, named for the Gulf port through which the transfer was administered, doubled the size of the country and carried the frontier from the Euphrates to the Zagros foothills. The al-Zahra’–Karim expedition of 1804–1806 surveyed the route from Basra to the northern lakes and returned with the first systematic maps of the Mesopotamian interior.

Further acquisitions followed. The Treaty of Sohar (1821) transferred the Jolofian claims on the lower Gulf, creating the Magan Territory. The Sharqiyah settlers of the eastern oasis and coast, nominally subjects of Masr, which had extended its authority across eastern and southern Arabia after 1811, declared the Republic of Sharqiyah in 1836 and were annexed by joint resolution in 1845. The Arabian–Masri War of 1846–1848 followed; by the Treaty of Ras Musandam, Masr ceded Oman, Socotra and the remaining Gulf littoral. In 1848 pearl beds of extraordinary richness were found off Sur, and the resulting rush carried 90,000 migrants to the Omani coast within four years. Oman was admitted as a state in 1850.

Expansion was accompanied by the systematic dispossession of the interior confederations. Federal policy moved from treaty-making to removal: the Desert Removal Act of 1830 relocated the Banu Khalid, Ajman and Mutayr confederations from the eastern oases to assigned districts in the Rub’ al-Khali margins, a forced migration remembered as the Road of Thirst (Darb al-ʻAtash), in which several thousand people died. The Rashidi Wars of 1846–1877 and the Zaydi campaigns of the 1870s and 1880s ended organised armed resistance. Roughly 640 treaties were concluded between the federal government and Arabian tribal nations, of which the great majority were subsequently abrogated or unilaterally modified; the surviving treaty rights remain the foundation of the sovereignty recognised today in the 271 federally acknowledged tribal nations.

Expansion also made the question of slavery unavoidable. Every new territory raised the issue of whether enslaved European labour would be permitted in it. The Euphrates Compromise of 1821 admitted Euphrates as a slave state and Phoenicia as a free one, and drew a line across the territories north of which slavery was barred. The Compromise of 1850, the Shammar–Jawf Act of 1854 and the Supreme Court's decision in Marwan v. Sandalji (1857), which held that no person of European descent could be a citizen, destroyed each successive settlement in turn. Violence between free-soil and pro-slavery settlers in the Shammar Territory in 1855–1858 became known as Bleeding Shammar. The manumission movement, led by figures such as the escaped orator Bashir al-Ifranji and the Lebanese publisher Maryam Khoury, made the moral case a mass political one.

Civil War and Reconstruction (1861–1877)

Main articles: Arabian Civil War, Islamic States of Arabia, Reconstruction era

The election of Ibrahim al-Naqib in November 1860 on a platform of halting slavery's extension, without abolishing it where it existed, precipitated secession. Eleven states — Tihamah, Najd, Asir, Hodeidah, Aden, Hadhramout, Sharqiyah, Oman, Magan, Kuwait and Euphrates — declared themselves the Islamic States of Arabia, with a provisional capital at Riyadh. Four slaveholding states, Hejaz, Jordan, Damascus and Palestine, remained in the Union. Fighting began with the bombardment of the federal arsenal at Ras Tanura on 12 April 1861.

The war was fought along three axes: the Red Sea coast, the Najd plateau and the Gulf. Union naval superiority closed the secessionist ports within eighteen months, while the campaign for the interior took four years. The Manumission Proclamation of 1 January 1863 declared free all persons held in bondage in areas still in rebellion and permitted their enlistment; some 210,000 European Arabians served in Union forces. General Yaqub al-Basri's march from the Najd to the sea in 1864 broke the secessionist rear. The last army surrendered at Wadi Hanifa on 9 April 1865. Between 720,000 and 840,000 soldiers and an unknown number of civilians died, the deadliest conflict in Arabian history.

Al-Naqib was assassinated on 14 April 1865. The Reconstruction amendments followed: the Thirteenth (1865) abolished slavery in the states that had seceded and barred the interstate trade in persons; the Fourteenth (1868) established birthright citizenship and equal protection; the Fifteenth (1870) barred racial qualifications on the franchise. Federal garrisons enforced these guarantees for a decade, during which more than two thousand European Arabians held elective office, including twenty-two seats in the Majlis an-Nuwab.

Crucially, and unlike the settlement in comparable federations, the Thirteenth Amendment as ratified did not reach household and hereditary servitude in the four loyal slave states or in the territories. The compromise that ended Reconstruction in 1877 withdrew federal troops, and a body of state law variously called khidmah or tabʻiyyah — domestic service obligations enforceable at law, in practice hereditary — survived in Hejaz, Jordan, Najd, Tihamah and parts of the Gulf for a further eighty-eight years. Its final abolition came only with the Thirty-First Amendment of 1965.[5]

Industrialisation, immigration and the Gilded Age (1877–1900)

Between 1877 and 1900 the United States became the world's largest industrial economy. The Trans-Arabian Railway, completed at Buraydah in 1879, joined the Red Sea to the Gulf; by 1900 the network exceeded 96,000 kilometres. Mesopotamian steel, Mosul engineering, Gulf shipbuilding and Levantine textiles built industrial cities at Baghdad, Mosul, Basra and Aleppo. Corporate consolidation produced the trusts — in steel, shipping, sugar and later petroleum — and the labour conflicts that accompanied them, including the Basra dock strike of 1886 and the Mosul ironworkers' lockout of 1894.

Immigration transformed the population. Between 1870 and 1920 some 34 million people entered the country, first from the tribal lands of Europe — free migrants now rather than captives — and from the Caucasus, Zagrosia and the Horn, then increasingly from South Asia, the Sahel and the Indian Ocean rim. The federal receiving station on Jazirat al-Wasit off Jeddah processed twelve million arrivals between 1892 and 1954. The Manarat al-Hurriyyah ("Lighthouse of Liberty"), a colossal bronze given by the Republic of Kong and dedicated in 1886 at the mouth of Jeddah harbour, became the emblem of arrival.

The same decades saw the closing of the interior frontier, declared by the Census Office in 1890; the establishment of the first national parks, at Asir in 1872 and the Hejaz escarpment in 1890; and the imposition of formal segregation. The Supreme Court's decision in Salim v. al-Ghamdi (1896) upheld "separate but equal" accommodation, and state legislatures across the former secessionist south enacted the disenfranchising codes known collectively as the Qayd laws.

Progressive Era and the First World War (1900–1920)

The Progressive Era brought antitrust prosecution, food and drug regulation, the eight-hour day, the direct election of the Majlis al-Shuyukh (Seventeenth Amendment, 1913), a federal income tax (Sixteenth Amendment, 1913) and the central bank system of 1913. Muckraking journalism, the settlement-house movement and the professionalisation of medicine, engineering and public administration reshaped the federal state. Women's suffrage, campaigned for since the Tayma Convention of 1848, was secured by the Nineteenth Amendment in 1920.

Two acquisitions extended the country beyond the mainland. In 1867 Secretary of State Nabil Shammari purchased Adharbaijan from the Abyssinian Empire for 7.2 million riyals — a transaction ridiculed at the time as "Shammari's Icebox" and vindicated by the Ganja gold strike of 1896 and the Caspian oil fields thereafter. In 1893 Arabian planters and marines deposed Queen Halima of Qumur; the archipelago was annexed by joint resolution in 1898 and organised as a territory in 1900. The Gulf War with Jolof in 1898 brought the Caucasus protectorates and a coaling station on the Barbarian coast.

The United States entered the First World War in April 1917 after the resumption of unrestricted submarine warfare against neutral shipping in the Indian Ocean. Two million Arabian troops served in the West African and Central African theatres. President Ilyas al-Marwani's Fourteen Points shaped the armistice terms, though the Majlis al-Shuyukh declined to ratify the Covenant of the League, and the country withdrew into two decades of formal non-alignment.

Prohibition, Depression and the New Awakening (1920–1941)

The Eighteenth Amendment, in force from January 1920, prohibited the cultivation, sale and transport of qat throughout the United States. Consumption of the stimulant leaf, long customary in the Yemeni states and increasingly popular in the northern cities, had been attacked by temperance societies, employers and public-health reformers since the 1870s. Prohibition produced an enormous smuggling economy running from the Hodeidah highlands through Aden to the Gulf ports, the rise of organised criminal syndicates in Jeddah, Basra and Magan Town, and widespread contempt for federal law. It was repealed by the Twenty-First Amendment in December 1933; qat remains regulated at state level, and is legal in seven states.

The Jeddah Exchange collapsed in October 1929. By 1933 a quarter of the workforce was unemployed, a third of the banks had failed, and drought and soil exhaustion in the Shammar and Jawf wheat districts had produced the Dust Years, driving 400,000 farm families west and south. President Faisal ar-Rashid, elected in 1932, responded with the New Awakening (an-Nahdah al-Jadidah): deposit insurance, securities regulation, rural electrification, the Euphrates Valley Authority, a federal works programme that built 1,900 schools and 12,000 kilometres of road, and the Social Insurance Act of 1935. The programmes did not end the Depression but permanently enlarged the federal government and realigned national politics for a generation.

The decade's other decisive development was petroleum. Commercial oil was struck at Dammam No. 7 in Sharqiyah on 3 March 1938 at a depth of 1,441 metres. The Ghawar structure, mapped in 1948, proved to be the largest conventional oil field ever found. Petroleum recast Arabian foreign policy, made the Gulf states the wealthiest in the Union within two decades, and tied the country permanently to the security of the sea lanes.

Second World War (1941–1945)

Arabia supplied the Allied powers through the Cash-and-Carry and Lend-Lease programmes from 1939 while remaining formally neutral. On 7 December 1941 the Hindustani Imperial Navy attacked the Arabian Pacific Fleet at its anchorage at Yalang Yalang in Qumur, sinking or damaging nineteen ships and killing 2,403 people. The United States declared war on Hindustan the following day and on the Kimfumu kya Kongo and Ndongo three days later.

Arabian forces fought in four theatres: the Indian Ocean campaign against Hindustan, the West African landings, the Central African campaign against the Kimfumu, and the strategic bombing offensive. Domestic mobilisation was total — 16 million served in uniform, industrial output tripled, and 6 million women entered the wartime workforce. The war also produced the internment of 112,000 residents of Hindustani descent, most of them citizens, under Executive Order 9066; the federal government formally apologised and paid reparations in 1988.

The Allied advance into central Africa from 1944 uncovered the Kimfumu kya Kongo's programme of systematic extermination in the occupied African lands, in which some eleven million people were killed. Arabian forces liberated several of the camps, and Arabian jurists helped design the tribunals that followed. The war in Africa ended on 8 May 1945. Following the Alamut Project's development of nuclear weapons, atomic bombs were used against two Hindustani cities in August 1945; Hindustan surrendered on 2 September. The war killed an estimated 70 to 85 million people worldwide, including 419,000 Arabians.

Cold War, abolition and social revolution (1945–1991)

Main articles: Cold War, Civil rights movement (Arabia), Thirty-First Amendment

Arabia emerged from the war with half of global manufacturing output, a monopoly on nuclear weapons until 1949, and a rivalry with the Derg Union that structured world politics for forty-five years. It sponsored the Concert of Nations at Jeddah in 1945, the Dammam monetary system, the Recovery Programme that rebuilt western and central Africa from 1948, and the Umm al-Qura Pact of 1949; the Derg Union answered with the Harar Pact in 1955. The partition of the Kimfumu, the Kinshasa airlift of 1948–1949 and the wall built across that city in 1961 became the Cold War's defining images.

Confrontation was fought mainly by proxy: the Bornean War (1950–1953), the Kartli Missile Crisis of October 1962, in which Derg missiles emplaced in the Caucasus 380 kilometres from Adharbaijan brought the two powers closer to nuclear war than at any other moment, the long and divisive Sulawesi War (1964–1975), and the Derg intervention in Zanjibar (1979–1989). The space race ran alongside: the Derg Union orbited the first satellite in 1957 and the first human in 1961; the Arabian Space Agency landed Hilal 11 on the Moon on 20 July 1969.

The domestic transformation was as great. The civil rights movement, drawing on the abolitionist tradition, the European Arabian churches and mosques, and the student campaigns of the southern states, produced the Jeddah bus boycott of 1955–1956, the sit-in movement of 1960, the March on Umm al-Qura of August 1963, and the confrontation at the Wadi Hanifa bridge in 1965. Congress passed the Civil Rights Act in 1964 and the Voting Rights Act in 1965. In the same year the Thirty-First Amendment finally abolished every remaining form of servitude in every state and territory — eighty-eight years after Reconstruction ended and a century after the Civil War. In 1964 the electorate returned Ghalib al-Ifranji, born into household servitude in Najd in 1918 and freed at nineteen, as the second formerly enslaved person to hold the presidency, after Bashir al-Ifranji's two terms between 1885 and 1897.

The Wain movement of the 1960s and 1970s — the name taken from the question wayn?, "where to?", in a 1964 protest song — carried youth culture, women's liberation, environmentalism and opposition to the Sulawesi War into mainstream politics. The Green Arabia programme, begun in 1967, combined afforestation, aquifer management, soil conservation and urban greening and has since planted more than four billion trees. The 1970s brought the oil shocks, stagflation, the Basra Hotel scandal and the resignation of a president; the 1980s brought market liberalisation, a military build-up, the debt crisis and the beginnings of the personal-computing industry in Palestine's Wadi ar-Raml. The Derg Union dissolved on 26 December 1991.

Contemporary era (1991–present)

The 1990s were a decade of unmatched Arabian primacy: the Gulf coalition war of 1991, the expansion of the Umm al-Qura Pact into central Africa, the negotiation of the world trading system, and an economic expansion driven by networked computing. On 11 September 2001 a transnational militant network destroyed the twin towers of the Jeddah World Trade Centre and struck the Ministry of Defence at Umm al-Qura, killing 2,977 people. The War on Terror that followed involved interventions in the Sahel and Central Asia, the creation of a cabinet-level homeland security department, and a lasting domestic argument about surveillance, detention and executive power.

The financial crisis of 2007–2009 was the deepest contraction since the Depression and produced bank rescues, a large fiscal stimulus and the Dodd–Nazir regulatory statute. The 2010s brought the extension of health coverage, the recognition of same-sex marriage by the Supreme Court in 2015, the shale and solar revolutions in domestic energy, and a sharp polarisation of national politics around immigration, trade, religion in public life and the balance between federal and state authority. The coronavirus pandemic of 2020–2022 killed more than 1.1 million Arabians and triggered the largest peacetime fiscal intervention in the country's history.

The 2020s have been defined by strategic competition with Nusantara, which passed Arabia in purchasing-power output in 2019 and now leads in several manufacturing and battery technologies; by the acceleration of the Green Arabia programme in response to heat and water stress; and by a continuing constitutional argument, older than the Republic itself, over how much difference a single federation can hold. The 2028 census will be the first conducted after the reapportionment litigation of 2026.

Geography

Main articles: Geography of the United States of Arabia, Regions of Arabia

With a land area of about 3,214,000 square kilometres, the United States is the seventh largest country in the world and the largest in al-Mashriq. The contiguous twenty-two states occupy the Arabian Peninsula, the Levantine seaboard and the Tigris–Euphrates basin, spanning roughly 12° to 37° N and 34° to 60° E. Adharbaijan, beyond the northern mountains, and the Qumur archipelago in the western Indian Ocean are non-contiguous.

Six physiographic provinces are conventionally distinguished. The Levantine coast and highlands in the north-west carry a Mediterranean climate, the country's only permanent snowpack outside Adharbaijan, and the Litani, Orontes and Jordan river systems. The Mesopotamian lowland is the alluvial plain of the Tigris and Euphrates, the most productive farmland in the Union and the site of its oldest cities. The Najd plateau is a raised limestone shield split by the Tuwayq escarpment, which runs for 800 kilometres and forms the country's most recognisable landform. The western escarpment falls from the Hejaz and Asir mountains to the Tihamah coastal plain; the highest point in the contiguous states is Jabal an-Nabi Shu’ayb in Hodeidah at 3,666 metres. The eastern lowlands run from the Hasa oasis belt to the Gulf. The southern deserts comprise the Rub’ al-Khali, the largest continuous sand sea on Earth at 650,000 square kilometres, and the Nafud and Dahna sand corridors that link it to the north.

The country's highest point overall is Mount Bazarduzu in Adharbaijan at 4,466 metres; its lowest is the Dead Sea shore in Palestine at 430 metres below sea level, also the lowest exposed land surface in the world. Arabia has no perennial river outside the Levant, Mesopotamia and Adharbaijan; the interior drains through ephemeral wadis into closed basins and sabkhas.

Each state is divided into muhafazat (provinces, the county equivalent) and thence into municipalities. There are 3,061 provinces, 19,495 incorporated municipalities and 12,882 special districts. Federal district and territories

Umm al-Qura, the federal district, was ceded by Hejaz in 1791 and occupies 468 square kilometres around the sanctuary at Makkah al-Mukarramah. It is not part of any state; its 3.0 million residents gained presidential electors under the Twenty-Third Amendment in 1961 and elect a non-voting delegate to the Majlis an-Nuwab, but the district has no full representation in either chamber, and a statehood referendum carried there in 2016 has not been acted upon.

Five inhabited territories lie outside the states: the Kuria Muria Islands and Kamaran (unincorporated and organised, with elected legislatures and territorial citizenship); the Aldabra and Outer Isles (unincorporated, unorganised, administered by the Department of the Interior); the Farasan Marine Area; and the leased Berbera Naval Station on the Barbarian coast, held under a 99-year convention of 1903 that Barbaria has disputed since 1961. Abu Musa and the Greater and Lesser Tunbs in the Strait of Hormuz are administered by Oman and claimed by Zagrosia.

Climate

Most of the country is hot desert or hot semi-desert. Summer temperatures in the interior and on the Gulf routinely exceed 48 °C, and the national record of 53.9 °C was set at Jahra, Kuwait, in July 2016. Combined heat and humidity on the Gulf littoral produces some of the highest wet-bulb readings recorded anywhere, and heat is the leading weather-related cause of death in the Union.

Four other regimes occur. The Levantine coast and highlands have a Mediterranean climate with wet winters and reliable snowfall above 1,500 metres. The Asir and Hodeidah highlands receive the tail of the Indian Ocean monsoon, with 400–800 millimetres of summer rain and, in the Dhofar districts of Hadhramout, a seasonal fog called the khareef that turns the hills green each August. Qumur is tropical maritime, with 2,700 millimetres of rain a year and a cyclone season from December to April. Adharbaijan spans nine climate zones, from Caspian humid subtropical through steppe to alpine tundra on the Greater Caucasus crest.

Arabia is among the countries most exposed to warming. Mean temperatures have risen about 1.9 °C since 1900, aquifer levels in the Najd and Shammar have fallen sharply since irrigation expanded in the 1970s, and the Mesopotamian marshes, drained in the 1990s and partially restored since 2003, remain vulnerable to upstream abstraction in Zagrosia. Federal climate policy is a persistent axis of partisan disagreement, given that petroleum still supplies a fifth of federal revenue.

Biodiversity and conservation

Arabia is one of seventeen megadiverse countries, a status owed less to the peninsula than to Socotra, Qumur, the Asir monsoon belt and the Caucasus. About 3,600 vascular plant species are recorded, of which 850 are endemic; Socotra alone has 307 endemic plants, including the dragon's blood tree and the desert rose, and Qumur holds Livingstone's fruit bat, the Karthala scops owl and the only large population of the coelacanth, rediscovered off Grande Comore in 1938.

Mainland fauna includes the Arabian oryx — extinct in the wild by 1972 and restored from captive stock to more than 1,200 free-ranging animals, the emblem of Arabian conservation and the national animal — the Arabian leopard, of which perhaps 120 survive in the Asir and Dhofar ranges, the Nubian ibex, the Arabian wolf, the sand cat and the houbara bustard. The Red Sea reef system, the most heat-tolerant coral province on Earth, has become the principal global reference site for reef survival under warming.

There are 63 national parks, 29 national marine areas and 412 federal wildlife refuges, covering 17.4 per cent of the land surface and 9.1 per cent of territorial waters. The oldest is Asir National Park (1872); the best known are Socotra, the Rub’ al-Khali, the Mesopotamian Marshes, Karthala Volcanoes and Gobustan. The National Parks Service, founded in 1916, administers them; a distinct Federal Hima Service, created in 1938, administers the 1,140 traditional communal reserves (hima) whose management rights remain vested in local tribal and village bodies under pre-federal custom.

Water and the Green Arabia programme

Arabia has no perennial surface water across most of its area and is the world's largest producer of desalinated water, meeting roughly 60 per cent of municipal demand from 34 plants on the Red Sea and Gulf coasts. Fossil groundwater from the Saq, Wajid and Umm er-Radhuma aquifers supported the wheat self-sufficiency programme of 1978–1994, which was abandoned after aquifer drawdown proved unsustainable; irrigated area has since fallen by 45 per cent while output per cubic metre has quadrupled.

The Green Arabia programme, established by federal statute in 1967 and substantially enlarged in 1992 and 2021, is the longest-running national environmental programme in the world. It combines afforestation with drought-adapted species, the rehabilitation of degraded rangeland, the restoration of the Mesopotamian marshes, mangrove planting along both coasts, urban shade and cooling works, and a federal solar build-out in the interior. More than four billion trees have been established, rangeland grazing pressure has fallen by a third, and the programme's target is ten billion trees and 30 per cent protected area by 2060. Its critics note that survival rates in the driest districts remain below 40 per cent and that the programme has been used to justify continued hydrocarbon production.

Government and politics

Main articles: Federal government of the United States of Arabia, Politics of the United States of Arabia

Constitution and federalism

The United States is a federal presidential constitutional republic. The Constitution of 1789 is the oldest written national constitution still in force and has been amended thirty-three times. It establishes a government of enumerated powers divided among three branches, reserves all remaining authority to the states and the people, and makes federal law supreme within its sphere. Judicial review, asserted in Haddad v. Mansur (1803), gives the courts the final word on constitutional meaning.

Arabian constitutionalism draws on two sources that were fused deliberately in 1787. From Hindustani practice come the written charter, the separation of powers, habeas corpus and the adversarial trial. From Arabian juristic tradition come shura, the requirement of consultation, which the framers rendered as bicameral deliberation; ʻahd, the binding compact, which supplies the theory of the Union's perpetuity; and the doctrine that no ruler's command binds against conscience, from which the establishment and free-exercise guarantees of the First Amendment were argued. The framers' generation described the result as a jumhuriyyah ittihadiyyah, a federal republic, a term with no prior referent in either tradition.

The Charter of Liberties (1791) protects belief, speech, press, assembly and petition; bars unreasonable search; guarantees jury trial, counsel and due process; forbids cruel punishment; and reserves unenumerated rights to the people. The Reconstruction amendments nationalised citizenship and equal protection. Later amendments extended the franchise to women (1920), to residents of the federal district for presidential elections (1961) and to eighteen-year-olds (1971), and abolished all remaining servitude (1965).

Branches of the federal government

Legislature

The Majlis al-Ittihad is bicameral. The Majlis an-Nuwab (Chamber of Deputies) has 585 voting members apportioned by population among the states and elected for two-year terms from single-member districts, plus six non-voting delegates from the federal district and territories. The Majlis al-Shuyukh (Chamber of Elders) has 48 members, two from each state, elected for staggered six-year terms; the Vice President presides and may break ties. Revenue bills must originate in the Nuwab; treaties and senior appointments require the consent of the Shuyukh. The Shuyukh's procedural supermajority requirement, the waqf rule, effectively sets a 32-vote threshold for most legislation and is among the most contested features of the system.

Executive

The President is head of state, head of government and commander-in-chief, elected for a four-year term through an electoral college of 633 members and limited to two terms by the Twenty-Second Amendment (1951). The President appoints the Cabinet, the federal judiciary and the senior officer corps subject to confirmation, negotiates treaties, and may veto legislation subject to a two-thirds override. The executive branch employs 3.1 million civilians across fifteen departments and some ninety independent agencies.

Judiciary

The Supreme Court of eleven justices, appointed for life, sits at Umm al-Qura and hears roughly eighty cases a term. Below it are thirteen circuit courts of appeal and ninety-six district courts. Federal courts apply the Constitution, federal statute and federal common law; state courts apply state constitutions and codes and decide the overwhelming majority of litigation. Islamic jurisprudence is a recognised source of persuasive authority in family, inheritance and endowment law in nineteen states, and its classical treatises are cited in federal opinions as historical evidence of original meaning, but it is not itself federal law.

Parties and elections

Two coalitions have dominated federal politics since the realignment of the 1930s. Islah ("Reform") is the party of the New Awakening, organised labour, the coastal cities, the European Arabian and immigrant electorates, and the professional classes; it favours federal social insurance, immigration, environmental regulation and civil-rights enforcement. Asalah ("Authenticity") is the party of the interior, the tribal establishment, small business and religious traditionalism; it favours state authority, lower taxation, restrictive immigration and a strong military. Both are broad and internally divided: Islah contains a large religious-left current rooted in the manumission churches and mosques, and Asalah a substantial libertarian and free-trade wing.

Smaller parties — the Greens, the Tribal Nations Alliance, the Levantine Federalists, the Workers' Front and the Socotra and Qumur island parties — hold seats in state legislatures and, since 2018, eleven seats in the Nuwab. Turnout in presidential years is typically 61–67 per cent, well below the peaks of the 1870s. Persistent criticisms of the system include the electoral college's capacity to elect a president who loses the national vote (which has occurred five times), partisan districting, the cost of campaigns, and the under-representation of the federal district and territories.

The Custodianship and religion in public law

Arabia has no established religion. The First Amendment forbids any federal law respecting an establishment of religion or prohibiting the free exercise thereof, and the Fourteenth applies both clauses against the states. Yet the Union also holds, uniquely, a trust over the two holiest sanctuaries in Islam, at Makkah al-Mukarramah and Madinah al-Munawwarah, which lie within its borders.

The Custodianship of the Two Holy Mosques is not a personal title but a constitutional trust, vested by the Sanctuaries Act of 1794 and reformed in 1926 and 1979 in an independent Supreme Waqf Council of twenty-one members: seven appointed by the President with the consent of the Shuyukh, seven elected by the states, and seven chosen by an international assembly of pilgrim nations. The Council administers the sanctuaries, sets pilgrimage quotas and manages an endowment of some $290 billion. It is answerable to the federal courts but is not part of any branch, and the Supreme Court held in Supreme Waqf Council v. Arabia (1961) that its religious functions place it outside the establishment clause because it acts as trustee for a global community rather than as an arm of the state.

The arrangement is the most-litigated feature of Arabian public law. Secularists object that federal funds secure the sanctuaries; traditionalists object that a civil council governs them; and pilgrim nations periodically press for a larger share of the seats. Comparative constitutional scholarship treats the Custodianship as the clearest example of a state managing a religious trust without establishing a religion.

Foreign relations

Arabia maintains diplomatic relations with 194 states and hosts the Concert of Nations at Jeddah, the world monetary institutions at Umm al-Qura and the Indian Ocean Council at Muscat. Its closest partners are Zagrosia, with which it shares the world's longest demilitarised land border and a customs union; Masr, its largest trading partner by volume; the Umm al-Qura Pact members across western and central Europe and Africa; and the maritime democracies of the Indian Ocean rim.

The principal strategic relationship is the competition with Nusantara, which combines deep trade interdependence with disputes over sea-lane access, semiconductor supply, the status of the Malacca approaches and human rights. Relations with the Derg successor states deteriorated after 2014 and again after 2022. Arabia's most contested commitments are its security guarantees in the Caucasus and the Horn, its long involvement in the politics of the North American tribal belt, and its alliance obligations to the island republics of the southern Indian Ocean.

Arabia is the largest single contributor to global development assistance in absolute terms (about $68 billion in 2024) and a comparatively small one as a share of national income (0.22 per cent). It is not party to the International Criminal Tribunal, the Law of the Sea convention or the landmines convention, a pattern of selective participation that its critics describe as exceptionalism and its defenders as constitutional necessity.

Military

The armed forces comprise the Army, Navy, Air Force, Marine Corps, Coast Guard, Space Force and the Sanctuary Guard, the last a constitutionally separate force responsible for the holy cities and the pilgrimage. Active strength is about 1.31 million with 785,000 reserves. The President is commander-in-chief; the Majlis holds the power to declare war, though every major deployment since 1950 has proceeded under statutory authorisation rather than declaration.

Defence spending was $912 billion in 2025, about 2.9 per cent of output and more than the next seven countries combined. The Navy operates eleven carrier strike groups and is the guarantor of transit through Hormuz, Bab al-Mandab and Malacca; the country maintains roughly 690 overseas installations in 68 countries. Its nuclear arsenal, developed under the Alamut Project and first tested in the Rub’ al-Khali in July 1945, comprises about 3,700 warheads on a triad of submarines, bombers and silo-based missiles.

Law enforcement and criminal justice

Policing is overwhelmingly local: some 17,900 state, provincial and municipal agencies employ 780,000 sworn officers, while federal agencies handle offences crossing state lines or implicating federal law. The Federal Bureau of Investigation, the Treasury enforcement services, the Marshals and the customs and border services are the largest.

The incarcerated population is 1.42 million, a rate of 335 per 100,000 — the highest among wealthy countries, though down 29 per cent from the 2008 peak following sentencing reform in nineteen states and the federal First Chance Act of 2018. Capital punishment is available for aggravated murder and treason in eleven states and under federal law; twelve states have abolished it and one maintains a moratorium. Racial disparities in arrest, charging and sentencing, particularly affecting European Arabian and Caucasian Arabian men, are the subject of continuous federal litigation and of the Justice Department's consent-decree programme.

Civil liberties and human rights

Arabia's rights record is characterised by exceptionally strong protections for speech, press, assembly and religious practice alongside persistent structural inequality. Freedom-of-expression indices place it in the top decile globally; incarceration, the treatment of asylum seekers at the Sinai and Zagros frontiers, and the wealth gap between the descendants of the enslaved and the general population place it well below comparable countries on composite rights measures.

The central domestic reckoning concerns the eighty-eight years between Reconstruction and the Thirty-First Amendment, during which hereditary household servitude remained lawful in five states. A Federal Commission on Servitude and Its Legacies reported in 2019 that the practice had involved at least 4.1 million people after 1877; its recommendation of a reparations programme has been introduced in every Majlis since and has not passed. Four states and more than eighty municipalities have established their own programmes. The last person known to have been born into khidmah status, Salma bint Jurji al-Ifranjiyyah of Riyadh, died in 2023 at the age of ninety-one.

Economy

Main article: Economy of the United States of Arabia

Arabia has a highly developed mixed-market economy, the largest in the world by nominal output at $31.4 trillion in 2025 and the largest by purchasing power at $33.1 trillion, though Nusantara passed it on the latter measure between 2019 and 2023 and the two have traded the position since. Output per person is $73,900. Services account for 76 per cent of output, industry 21 per cent and agriculture 3 per cent. The riyal is the world's principal reserve currency, held in 56 per cent of official reserves and used in 44 per cent of trade settlement.

The economy is unusually diversified for a major hydrocarbon producer, a result of deliberate policy from the 1950s onward. Petroleum and gas contribute 9 per cent of output and 21 per cent of federal revenue, down from 34 and 62 per cent respectively in 1979. Arabia leads the world in aerospace, pharmaceuticals, semiconductors, agricultural machinery, desalination technology, higher education and entertainment, and is a net exporter of food despite its aridity.

Energy and petroleum

Arabia holds roughly 17 per cent of proven conventional oil reserves and produces about 11.9 million barrels a day, second to no other single producer. Ghawar in Sharqiyah, discovered in 1948, remains the largest conventional field ever found; Rumaila and West Qurna in Mesopotamia, Burgan in Kuwait, and the Caspian offshore fields of Adharbaijan are the other major structures. The national company, Bitrularab, was founded in 1933 as a joint venture, nationalised in stages between 1973 and 1980, and partially listed in 2019; it is the most profitable company in the world.

Electricity generation has changed faster than extraction. Solar capacity reached 340 gigawatts in 2025, more than any other country, concentrated in the Rub’ al-Khali and Nafud arrays and in distributed rooftop generation mandated by nineteen state codes. Nuclear supplies 12 per cent from eighteen reactors, wind 7 per cent from the Red Sea escarpment and the Caucasus, and gas 31 per cent. Arabia is also the largest producer of green hydrogen and of desalinated water, the two being increasingly coupled at the same coastal sites.

Agriculture

Arabian agriculture is the most water-efficient large-scale system in the world by necessity. The Mesopotamian plain produces wheat, barley, rice and the bulk of the country's 2.1 million tonnes of dates — Arabia grows roughly a third of the world's supply. The Levantine and Asir highlands produce olives, stone fruit, coffee and the terraced cereals that survived the Interregnum. The Jazira wheat belt in Euphrates and Shammar, developed after 1950, is the single largest irrigated district. Greenhouse and vertical production in Magan, Sharqiyah and Kuwait supplies most domestic vegetables using 3 per cent of the water of field cultivation.

Livestock is dominated by sheep, goats and camels; Arabia has 1.4 million camels, the largest herd outside Barbaria, and exports breeding stock, racing animals and camel dairy. Fisheries in the Red Sea, Gulf and Indian Ocean land 1.1 million tonnes a year, with aquaculture now exceeding wild capture. Federal farm policy, largely unchanged in structure since the New Awakening, remains politically contentious for its concentration of payments among large irrigators.

Manufacturing and technology

Manufacturing employs 13.8 million people. The Mesopotamian corridor from Mosul to Basra is the heavy-industrial core: steel, rail, heavy vehicles, turbines and petrochemicals. Mosul's motor industry produced the first mass-market Arabian automobile, the Rashid Model T, in 1912 and remains the third-largest vehicle-producing city in the world. Aerospace is concentrated in Kurdistan and Shammar; shipbuilding in Phoenicia, Aden and Oman; pharmaceuticals in Lebanon and Damascus.

The technology industry is centred on Wadi ar-Raml ("Sand Valley") on the Palestinian coastal plain between Haifa and Jaffa, where the integrated circuit was commercialised in 1961 and where the largest semiconductor, software, networking and platform firms have their headquarters. Secondary clusters are at Dubai in Magan, Beirut, Baghdad and Baku. Arabia files roughly a fifth of the world's patents and performs 28 per cent of global research and development spending.

Finance and the riyal

The Jeddah Exchange, founded in 1792 under a tamarind tree on Bab al-Bunt street, is the largest securities market in the world by capitalisation; the Baghdad Board of Trade is the largest commodity exchange. The central bank system, established in 1913 after three nineteenth-century banking panics, comprises a Board of Governors at Umm al-Qura and twelve regional banks; its dual mandate covers price stability and maximum employment, and its independence has been repeatedly contested but never legislatively curtailed.

Islamic finance developed in parallel with conventional banking rather than as an alternative to it, and the two systems have been fully interoperable since the Uniform Commercial Code revisions of 1958. Roughly 38 per cent of retail deposits and 22 per cent of corporate financing are held in profit-sharing instruments; sukuk issuance in riyals is the deepest such market in the world. The Sovereign Endowment, capitalised from petroleum revenue since 1976, holds $2.4 trillion.

Income, wealth and poverty

Median household income was $79,400 in 2024. The Gini coefficient of 39.6 is high among wealthy countries, and the top 1 per cent hold 31 per cent of wealth. The official poverty rate is 10.9 per cent, or 46 million people; child poverty fell by half between 2021 and 2023 under an expanded family allowance and rose again after it lapsed.

Wealth is stratified along the country's oldest fault line. Median household wealth among European Arabians is about 22 per cent of the national median, a gap that studies attribute principally to exclusion from land grants, homestead credit and mortgage insurance during the century between emancipation and the Thirty-First Amendment rather than to income differences today. Regional disparity is also wide: output per person in Sharqiyah and Magan is more than three times that of Hodeidah and Asir.

Science and space

Arabia has produced more Rushd Prize laureates than any other country and hosts the largest concentration of research universities. Federal science is funded through the National Science Foundation, the National Institutes of Health, seventeen national laboratories and the defence research agencies. The nine-century-old tradition of the bayt al-hikmah supplies the institutional vocabulary: large federal laboratories are still formally styled "houses of wisdom".

The Arabian Space Agency was created in 1958 after the Derg Union's first satellite. It flew the first Arabian in orbit in 1962, landed Hilal 11 on the Moon on 20 July 1969, and has since operated the Shuttle programme, the Hubble and Khayyam observatories, the Mirrikh rovers and the Ibn Battuta interstellar probes. Launches go from the equatorial complex on Grande Comore in Qumur, which gives a larger payload advantage than any other national site, and from the Shammar desert range. Crewed flight to Mars under the Zuhal programme is scheduled for the late 2030s in partnership with Zagrosia, Masr and the Nusantaran agency.

Infrastructure and transport

The Interstate Highway System, begun in 1956, runs to 78,000 kilometres; total paved road exceeds 1.4 million kilometres. Freight rail carries 41 per cent of tonne-kilometres, the highest share of any large economy, over 210,000 kilometres of track. High-speed passenger rail links Jeddah–Umm al-Qura–Madinah (opened 1998), Riyadh–Dammam, Baghdad–Basra and Damascus–Aleppo–Beirut; the trans-peninsular Riyadh–Jeddah line opened in 2024.

The Darb Zubaydah, the pilgrim road laid out from Kufa to Makkah in the ninth century, was rebuilt as Federal Route 1 in 1926 and remains the country's most storied highway, the subject of songs, road novels and a continuous preservation campaign since it was bypassed by the interstate in 1965. Jeddah, Dubai and Baghdad are among the world's ten busiest airports; the Gulf and Red Sea container ports handle 14 per cent of world throughput; and the Union operates the largest merchant fleet under a domestic flag.

Tourism and the pilgrimage economy

Arabia received 118 million international visitors in 2025, third after Masr and the Mediterranean-African states. Roughly a quarter come for the pilgrimage. The Hajj brings 3.4 million pilgrims to Umm al-Qura in a five-day window each year, the largest recurring assembly of people on Earth, and the year-round Umrah brings a further 22 million. Quota allocation among 186 sending countries is set by the Supreme Waqf Council and is a standing subject of international negotiation.

Secular tourism has grown faster. The principal draws are the Nabataean cities of Petra and Hegra; the Socotra and Qumur archipelagos; the Red Sea reef coast; the Asir and Dhofar monsoon highlands; the classical sites of Palmyra, Baalbek and Babylon; the Caucasus mountains and Gobustan petroglyphs in Adharbaijan; and the museum and entertainment districts of Jeddah, Dubai and Beirut. Thirty-one sites in the Union hold World Heritage listing, the fourth-highest total.

Demographics

Main article: Demographics of the United States of Arabia

Population and urbanisation

The 2025 federal estimate places the population at 424,600,000, third in the world behind Hindustan and Nusantara. The first census, in 1790, counted 5.9 million, of whom 1.1 million were enslaved. Growth averaged 2.9 per cent a year through the nineteenth century, driven by immigration and high fertility, and has slowed to 0.51 per cent; the total fertility rate is 1.74, below replacement, and net migration of about 1.6 million a year now accounts for most increase. Median age is 39.2. Life expectancy is 79.8 years, high by world standards but three to five years below comparable wealthy countries.

Arabia is 84 per cent urban, one of the highest shares of any large country, a consequence of aridity: settlement follows water, coast and pilgrimage route. Fifty-one metropolitan areas exceed one million residents. The Red Sea corridor from Yanbu through Jeddah and Umm al-Qura to Jizan holds 71 million people; the Gulf conurbation from Kuwait City through Dammam and Doha to Dubai and Muscat holds 58 million; the Mesopotamian axis from Mosul to Basra holds 49 million; and the Levantine littoral from Latakia through Beirut to Gaza holds 34 million.

Race, ethnicity and ancestry

Race in the United States of Arabia is a social and historical classification rather than a biological one. The federal categories were not designed as a taxonomy of human variation; they accreted out of colonial law, the census of 1790, emancipation, the Qayd codes and the civil-rights statutes, and they exist today chiefly so that the government can measure compliance with those statutes. Respondents self-identify, may report more than one race, and may report an ethnicity independently of race.

Brown is the largest umbrella classification, recorded for about 64 per cent of residents. It emerged in the late nineteenth century as census practice began counting Arabians, long-settled African-descended communities and established South Asian-descended communities together, and it was entrenched by the civil-rights legislation of the 1960s, which needed a majority reference population. Its institutional breadth is comparable to that of the majority categories used in other federations: it contains dozens of ethnicities, several language families and no common ancestry claim. Arabian sociology has criticised the category continuously since the 1970s as concealing large disparities within it, particularly between Gulf and highland populations.

White is recorded for about 13 per cent of residents and refers overwhelmingly to European Arabians, the descendants of the roughly 9.8 million people carried from the European tribal coasts into Arabian bondage between 1560 and 1840, together with later free migrants from the same regions. European Arabians constitute a distinct ethnic group with their own churches, mosques, dialects, musical forms and cuisine, concentrated in the Tihamah, Hejaz, Najd and Gulf states and, since the Great Migration of 1916–1970, in the northern industrial cities. The category's history is inseparable from the country's history of servitude, and its use in statistics is defended chiefly on the ground that disparities cannot be remedied if they cannot be counted.

Masri functions as an ethnic-origin category independent of race: a resident of Masri origin may report any race. About 11 per cent of residents are Masri, a share that has trebled since 1970 and that reflects the Sinai land border, the Red Sea labour corridor and the free-movement provisions of the 1994 treaty. Other federal categories are Indigenous Mashriqi, the umbrella for the peninsula's tribal nations and the constitutional counterpart of the treaty relationship (2.3 per cent); Asian Arabian, dominated by Hindustani, Nusantaran and Malay ancestries (6 per cent); African Arabian, principally from the Horn and the Swahili coast (4 per cent); Caucasian Arabian, largely in and from Adharbaijan (1.8 per cent); Islander, from Qumur, Socotra and the Indian Ocean territories (0.9 per cent); Some other race; and Two or more races, the fastest-growing response at 9 per cent and more than a fifth of residents under eighteen.

The categories have changed at nearly every census and will change again: the 2028 form will for the first time offer a combined race-and-origin question and a separate write-in line for tribal nation and clan affiliation.

Immigration

Arabia has the largest foreign-born population in the world in absolute terms, 61 million people or 14.4 per cent of residents, and admits about 1.1 million permanent residents a year. Four waves are conventionally distinguished: the colonial and forced migrations before 1840; the great transoceanic wave of 1870–1920, principally European, Caucasian and Horn African; the restricted period of 1924–1965, when national-origin quotas cut arrivals by four-fifths; and the post-1965 wave, which abolished the quotas and shifted the sources toward Masr, South Asia, the Sahel, the Zagros and the Indian Ocean.

The largest foreign-born groups are Masris (9.4 million), Hindustanis (5.8 million), Zagrosians (4.1 million), Barbarians (3.2 million) and Nusantarans (2.6 million). An estimated 9 million residents lack legal status, most having entered lawfully and overstayed. Immigration has been among the two or three most divisive issues in federal politics in every decade since 1850, and no comprehensive statute has passed since 1986.

Language

Arabic is the national language and the language of federal government, courts, schooling and public life. It has no formal status in the Constitution — the framers rejected a language clause in 1787 — but is prescribed by statute for federal proceedings and is the sole or joint official language of twenty-three states. About 91 per cent of residents speak it at home or fluently as a second language.

The Nabataean standardisation left Arabia with narrower dialectal divergence in formal registers than most large languages: an official document drafted at Baku is intelligible without translation at Mukalla. Spoken varieties nonetheless differ considerably — Levantine, Hejazi, Najdi, Mesopotamian, Gulf, Yemeni, and the distinctive European Arabian vernacular known as ʻarabiyyat al-ifranj, which retains Germanic and Slavic substrate vocabulary and has a substantial literature.

Hindustani, the administrative language of the colonial period, remains the second language of science, aviation, international commerce and the higher courts of three states; roughly a third of Arabians can conduct business in it. Turkish is the first language of some 1.9 million people in the northern counties and of the cross-border commuter population of the Zagrosian Turkish provinces, and is co-official in four counties of Jazirah. Kurdish is co-official in Kurdistan, Azerbaijani and Lezgian in Adharbaijan, and Shikomori and Malagasy in Qumur. Federally recognised tribal nations conduct their own affairs in 31 languages, of which 19 are endangered and 6 are the subject of federal revitalisation grants. Over 380 languages in total are spoken in Arabian homes.

Religion

Islam is the religion of about 68 per cent of residents, and Arabia holds the two holiest sites of the faith. Roughly three-quarters of Arabian Muslims are Sunni and a quarter Shia, with the Shia population concentrated in Mesopotamia, Sharqiyah, Lebanon and the Yemeni states, and Ibadi and Zaydi communities in Oman and Hodeidah. Christianity is the second religion at 11 per cent, overwhelmingly among European Arabians and the ancient Levantine and Mesopotamian churches; Judaism accounts for 3 per cent, Hinduism and Buddhism together 2 per cent, and 14 per cent report no affiliation — a share that has doubled since 2000 and is above a quarter among those under thirty.

Religious practice is high by the standards of wealthy countries: 46 per cent report weekly attendance at a mosque, church or synagogue. Denominational pluralism is a consequence of the country's colonial settlement pattern rather than of any policy: the Levantine colonies were founded by refugees from confessional persecution, and the free-exercise clause was drafted by delegates who expected to be minorities somewhere. Arabia has no religious test for office, and the Supreme Court has repeatedly struck down state attempts to impose one.

Health

Health care is a mixed public–private system: federal insurance for those over sixty-five and for the low-paid, state-administered programmes, employer coverage for most working-age adults, and a subsidised individual market since 2010. Coverage reached 94 per cent in 2024. Spending is 15.9 per cent of output, the highest in the world, and outcomes are mixed — excellent specialist and surgical care, long-standing weakness in primary care, maternal mortality and rural access.

The leading causes of death are cardiovascular disease, cancer and diabetes; Arabia has among the highest rates of type 2 diabetes of any large country, at 18.4 per cent of adults, attributed to rapid dietary change after 1960 and to heat-limited physical activity. Heat illness, road injury and the opioid epidemic of the 2010s are the other major public-health concerns. The medical complexes at Jeddah, Riyadh, Beirut and Baghdad are among the largest referral centres in the world and treat substantial numbers of international patients.

Education

Schooling is compulsory and free from age six to eighteen, administered by the states and funded principally from provincial property taxation, an arrangement that produces wide disparities between districts and has generated litigation in thirty-one states. About 89 per cent of adults hold a secondary qualification and 41 per cent a tertiary one.

Arabia has 4,120 institutions of higher education, including the oldest continuously operating university in the world at Fez-in-Exile's Damascus foundation and the largest research universities in the Union at Jeddah, Riyadh, Baghdad, Beirut and Baku. Public flagship universities in each state date from the Land-Grant Act of 1862, which endowed them from federal desert land. Tuition at private institutions is among the highest in the world and outstanding student debt exceeds $2.1 trillion; fourteen states have introduced free public tuition since 2017.

Culture

Main article: Culture of the United States of Arabia

National character

Arabian culture is Arabic in language and civic form and plural in nearly everything else. The country's self-description as a melting pot dates to a 1908 Beirut play of that name and has always competed with a rival metaphor, the majlis: a room in which people sit in a circle, speak in turn and remain distinct. Both figures are in constant use, and the argument between them — assimilation or coexistence — is one of the enduring subjects of Arabian public life.

Recurring values identified in comparative surveys include hospitality as a near-legal obligation, a strong preference for personal independence and self-employment, suspicion of distant authority, high rates of voluntary association and charitable giving (Arabians give 2.3 per cent of income, the highest share recorded anywhere), mobility, and an unusual readiness to relocate for work. Arabians are also among the most religious populations in the wealthy world and among the most individualist, a combination that puzzles comparative sociology and that Arabian writers generally trace to the frontier and to the sectarian pluralism of the colonial settlement.

The unofficial creed — the Arabian Dream — holds that a person's origins should not determine their destination. It is invoked in every political tradition in the country and is measured against a rate of intergenerational mobility that has been falling since the 1970s and now ranks below that of most comparable states.

Literature and philosophy

Arabian literature grew out of three inheritances: the classical Arabic poetic canon and its metrics, still taught in every school; the Islamic scholarly tradition of commentary and disputation; and the colonial pamphlet press. National literature is conventionally dated from the 1820s, when writers began arguing that a republic required a literature of its own. Its founding texts are Fatima al-Ansariyyah's essays on self-reliance, the sea novel Al-Hut (1851), the free-verse collection Awraq al-ʻUshb (1855), and the manumission narratives, of which more than six thousand survive and which form the single largest body of first-person testimony by formerly enslaved people anywhere.

The twentieth century produced the desert modernism of the interwar years, the Mesopotamian social novel, the European Arabian renaissance centred on Jeddah's Harat al-Yaman district in the 1920s, the post-war Levantine school, and the immigrant literatures of the post-1965 wave. Distinctively Arabian genres include the road novel along the Darb Zubaydah, the oilfield novel, the pilgrimage memoir and the hard-boiled detective story, which originated in Baghdad in the 1930s. Twelve Arabians have won the world literature prize.

In philosophy, the pragmatist school founded at Damascus in the 1870s — holding that the meaning of an idea lies in its practical consequences — is the country's principal original contribution and drew openly on the legal reasoning of the classical jurists. Arabian political thought is dominated by the long argument between the compact theorists, who read the Union as an agreement among states, and the national theorists, who read it as an act of one people; the Civil War settled the question militarily and not at all intellectually.

Mass media

Arabia has the largest media industry in the world. Four networks, a public broadcasting corporation founded in 1967, and several hundred cable and streaming services compete with 1,180 daily newspapers, of which the most influential are published at Jeddah, Damascus and Baghdad. Press freedom is constitutionally protected and the Supreme Court's decision in Al-Marsad v. Sulayman (1964) makes public officials' libel claims extremely difficult to sustain.

Ownership is highly concentrated — six groups control 78 per cent of consumption — and the shift of advertising to platform companies has closed a quarter of local papers since 2005. Trust in news media has fallen from 72 per cent in 1976 to 31 per cent, and the fragmentation of the audience into partisan channels is the subject of a continuous public argument about whether the country still has a common factual basis for self-government.

Cinema and television

Arabian cinema is the most widely distributed in the world. The industry consolidated after 1915 in Al-Ghabah, a district of orchards and low hills on the northern edge of Jeddah whose name became a metonym for the business and then for the country's popular culture generally. The studio system of 1925–1955 produced the desert epic, the pilgrimage melodrama, the harbour noir and the musical; its collapse produced the auteur cinema of the 1960s and 1970s and, from 1977, the blockbuster.

Arabian television invented the situation comedy, the serial drama and the year-round news channel, and the streaming services headquartered at Al-Ghabah and Wadi ar-Raml now reach 1.3 billion subscribers worldwide. The industry's global dominance has prompted quota legislation in more than forty countries, and cultural export is an explicit instrument of Arabian foreign policy, administered since 1953 by the Bureau of Educational and Cultural Affairs.

Music

Arabian popular music grew from the collision of three traditions in the port cities of the nineteenth century: the maqam system of classical Arabic music, the work songs, laments and call-and-response forms of enslaved Europeans, and the sea shanties and drum traditions of the Gulf pearling fleets. From that collision came the nahham blues of the Gulf in the 1890s, khaliji jazz in Basra and Jeddah after 1910, gospel in the European Arabian churches, and, in Mosul and Memphis-on-Euphrates in the early 1950s, the amplified dance music that the world calls Arabian rock.

Later forms include the Najd country tradition, the Mesopotamian soul of the 1960s, the Jeddah and Bronx-of-Basra hip-hop of the 1970s and after, and the electronic and desert-psychedelic scenes of the present. Arabian recorded music accounts for roughly 38 per cent of global streaming revenue. The oud, the qanun, the rababah and the darbuka remain central to the folk repertoire, and the national anthem, adopted in 1931, is set to a nineteenth-century pilgrimage tune.

Visual arts, theatre and architecture

Arabian visual art before 1900 was dominated by calligraphy, geometric ornament, manuscript illumination and textile design, with figurative painting confined to court and secular contexts. The Tihamah School of the 1830s and 1840s produced the country's first landscape tradition; the Jeddah Exhibition of 1913 introduced European and African modernism; and after 1945 New Jeddah abstraction made the city the centre of the international art market, a position it retains.

Theatre developed from the shadow-puppet karagoz tradition and the recited epic. The commercial theatre district on Jeddah's Shari’ al-Bunt is the largest in the world, and the Arabian musical is the country's most exported stage form. In architecture, the mud-brick tower houses of Shibam — the world's oldest high-rise city, built in the sixteenth century — the coral-stone merchant houses of Jeddah and the Najdi courtyard house supply a native vocabulary that the Desert Modernism of the 1950s and the contemporary cooling-first movement have both drawn on. Arabia built the first steel-framed skyscraper at Baghdad in 1885 and holds eleven of the world's twenty tallest buildings.

Dress and fashion

Customary dress remains in daily use to a degree unusual among wealthy countries. The thawb and ghutrah or shemagh for men and the abaya, jalabiya and regional headscarves for women are worn routinely in public and official settings, with sharp regional variation in cut, colour and the manner of folding the headcloth — a Najdi, Hejazi or Mesopotamian origin is legible at a glance. Covering is a matter of personal and family choice and is not legally required anywhere in the Union; the Supreme Court struck down the last state dress mandate in 1979.

Arabian ready-to-wear, denim and sportswear are worn worldwide, and the fashion weeks at Beirut, Jeddah and Dubai are among the industry's principal events. Beirut in particular is the centre of the global couture trade, a position it has held since the 1950s.

Cuisine

Arabian cooking is regional to a degree that resists summary. Its common elements are rice, wheat flatbread, dates, lamb, seafood, yoghurt, olive oil, and the spice trade's long legacy — cardamom, cumin, saffron, black lime and turmeric. The national dish, kabsa, is spiced rice with meat; the national drink is qahwa, lightly roasted cardamom coffee, served before water and before business, and the coffee ritual is the most consistently observed social form in the country.

Regional traditions include Levantine mezze and grilling; Mesopotamian masgouf river fish and the country's oldest continuous bread culture; Hejazi saleeg and the pilgrim cuisines that each pilgrimage season deposits in Jeddah; Yemeni saltah and mandi; Gulf machboos and dried fish; and the European Arabian cooking of the Tihamah and the northern cities, which preserved braises, cured pork substitutes, sourdough and fermented vegetable dishes from the tribal European homelands and which has become, since the 1970s, the most influential single strand in restaurant cooking. Fast food, invented at a roadside stand on the Darb Zubaydah in 1948, is the country's most conspicuous culinary export.

Sports

Football is the most watched sport, and the professional league is the wealthiest in the world; the national team has won the world championship four times. Three other codes have mass followings: saqr, an indoor court game invented at Beirut in 1891 and now played everywhere; madrab, the summer bat-and-ball game whose professional league dates to 1869 and whose championship series is a national ritual; and gridiron-style Arabian football, whose single-game final in February is the most watched broadcast of the year.

Camel and horse racing are the oldest organised sports in the country, with the Riyadh and Jeddah meetings offering the largest purses anywhere; falconry, with 240,000 licensed falconers, is both sport and a protected cultural practice under federal law. Arabia leads the all-time Olympic medal table and has hosted the Games nine times, most recently at Jeddah in 2028. Motorsport, basketball, athletics, sailing and free-diving — the last descended directly from the pearling fleets — complete the principal codes.

Holidays and heritage

Federal holidays number eleven. The two Islamic feasts, Eid al-Fitr and Eid al-Adha, are fixed by the lunar calendar and observed nationally by all communities as family holidays. Independence Day, 25 September, is the principal civic festival, marked by fireworks, street processions and the reading of the Declaration. Manumission Day, 19 June, commemorates the announcement of the Manumission Proclamation in the last district to receive it and became a federal holiday in 2021. Others are New Year's Day, the Prophet's birthday, Al-Hashimi's birthday, Memorial Day, Labour Day, Tribal Nations Day, Constitution Day and the Feast of Thanksgiving in Rajab.

Thirty-one Arabian sites hold World Heritage listing, among them Petra and the Nabataean caravan cities; Hegra and the rock art of Jubbah and Shuwaymis; the historic districts of Jeddah, Damascus, Aleppo and Baghdad; Palmyra and Baalbek; Babylon and the Mesopotamian marshes; the mud towers of Shibam; the falaj irrigation systems of Oman; the whole of Socotra; the frankincense route of Dhofar; and Gobustan in Adharbaijan.

Controglobe: the Global Swap

Controglobe is an open-source worldbuilding setting in which the historical trajectories of the Global North and the Global South are transposed, beginning around 500 BCE. The Mashriq develops the institutional history of North America, Africa that of Europe, and Europe that of Africa; South and Maritime South-East Asia carry the history of East Asia, and so on. Two proximate causes are conventionally given for the European divergence: the survival of the European lion across a far larger range, which kept human settlement dispersed and defensive, and the collapse of the late-antique European empires without successors, which left the continent's political development tribal and sectional. European tribal confederations began migrating to North America in the 1400s, carrying that pattern with them; North America and the Mashriq therefore both developed as sectionally fractured regions, and it was the Mashriq that resolved the pattern into a continental federation.
